Amazon Category Intelligence: Where Demand Meets the Catalog

A U.S. automotive aftermarket seller lists about 580 alternators, starters and NOx sensors on Amazon but sells only a few units a month. Is the problem demand, price, stock, or how the listings are written — and what should be fixed first?

CategoriesAlternators · starters · NOx sensors
DataProduct Opportunity Explorer · All Listings · order history
MethodPart-number matching (LESTER / OE) in DuckDB SQL
OutputSix fixes, ranked by addressable demand

The short answer

Demand is not the bottleneck, nor is pricing in most cases. The seller already stocks exact-match components for 3,994 competitor clicks a month, priced within 15% of the market on all 12, and has sold 1 unit of them in 22 months. Buyers search by part number and model year, and these listings don't say either. The first fixes are writing, restocking and verifying fitment. Buying more traffic comes last.

1 · How the analysis moved

Nov 3 – 11, 2026 · each step's actual output
  1. Nov 3

    Baseline the catalog and the sales

    Before looking at the market, I pulled the seller's full listing report and 22 months of orders (Windsor.ai, with Claude doing the pulls), so every later finding could be checked against what actually sells.

  2. Nov 4

    Pull demand, a different way per category

    No single Amazon tool covered all three categories. Alternators had no unmet-demand entry, so I used matched products from seed ASINs. Starters had a clear unmet-demand list. NOx returned nothing, so I switched methods.

  3. Nov 4

    Match competitors to the catalog by part number

    I grouped the 303 alternator competitors by LESTER interchange number and joined them to 497 listings. Each match got a status, a price gap and a specific listing fix, not just a score.

  4. Nov 6

    Go deeper where the first pass was thin

    The first NOx pull found only two competitors. Ten more seed exports later, every listed NOx part had a same-part rival. That turned a hunch about price into evidence.

  5. Nov 10

    Reconcile with what actually sells

    Demand data and the order log have to agree. Making the unit totals tie exposed the analysis's blind spot: most real sales come from parts the market tools never surfaced.

  6. Nov 10–11

    Stress-test, then turn findings into actions

    I reviewed the results from three angles (marketplace, aftermarket parts and paid search), then built a ranked action tab. Nothing changes in the account without approval.

Paid traffic follows these fixes, not the other way round. In the funnel & attribution analysis, ads sent shoppers to listings that couldn't close. Next step: the lifecycle matrix decides which of these parts deserve stock over the next three to five years.

How it's built

Method

  • Demand: Product Opportunity Explorer niche pages for category size, "matched products" from seed ASINs for part-level competitor clicks, and unmet-demand lists for starter search intents.
  • Matching: each competitor's LESTER or OE part number joined to the seller's All Listings report, with every result labelled exact part (in stock or delisted), same vehicle under a different part, or not carried.
  • Outcome: 22 months of order lines joined back by ASIN, so demand, price gap and actual sales sit in one row.

Limits, stated plainly

  • Competitor clicks show where demand is, not what the seller would capture.
  • Matched-product lists are Amazon's similarity picks, not the full market, and the seeds skew toward GM and Asian models.
  • Fitment matches marked "same vehicle" still need an engineering check.
  • The seller's own prices are shown only as gaps; no revenue figures are published.
