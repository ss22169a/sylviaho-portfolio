ICE Parts Lifecycle: Which SKUs to Invest In, Harvest or Exit

Electric cars are taking share of new sales, but alternators, starters and NOx sensors are bought for cars that are already on the road. For one U.S. aftermarket seller's catalog: where is the last profitable window, and where should restocking stop?

Scope96 demand lines · alternators, starters, NOx sensors
DataNHTSA survival schedules · Amazon demand · seller catalog
MethodLifecycle stage × demand rank in DuckDB SQL
OutputA four-way SKU decision, plus a price override

Conclusion

EV adoption poses little near-term risk to this catalog: with all fitments at least 4 years old, the underlying installed base is fully established. The risk is that the stock sits in the wrong part of the lifecycle. Of 24 in-stock lines, 9 fall in Exit (old fleets, weak demand), 8 are NOx sensors priced 72%–273% above same-part rivals, and only 1 sits in Invest. Meanwhile 19 of the 20 Invest lines are parts the seller doesn't carry. Restocking should move from 1990s–2000s fitments toward 2010s light trucks and SUVs.

What this means for a parts seller: EVs change what gets built from now on. They don't remove the cars already sold, and those cars are getting older, which is when alternators and starters fail.

2 · Turning vehicle age into a lifecycle stage

NHTSA DOT HS 809 952 · grade A

NHTSA's survival schedules give the share of each model year still on the road at each age. Each demand line gets the share for its midpoint model year, and that share sets its stage: Ramp (85%+ still running), Prime (45–85%), Tail (15–45%) and Sunset (under 15%).

  • Passenger cars
  • Light trucks / SUVs (also used for heavy trucks as a conservative proxy)

The schedules date from 2006, and cars now last longer (the fleet average has kept rising), so the stages are conservative: a line marked Tail may still have more road left. That is one reason demand, not age alone, decides the action.

5 · Checking the assumption: does demand fade with age?

If older fitments were dying off, their search volume should be falling faster. For 23 starter searches with a model year, median year-on-year search growth by stage:

Prime11 searches
-5%
Tail10 searches
-6%
Sunset2 searches
+15%

It doesn't, at least not yet. Prime and Tail searches shrink at about the same rate, and the two Sunset searches are growing (only two, so treat that as noise). The correlation between vehicle age and search growth is +0.42. Age tells you which way a line is heading; current demand tells you whether it still pays. That is why the matrix uses both.

Why the plan changed during the analysis

  • Dropped: a fixed "7–13 year repair sweet spot". No grade A or B source supported a single window, so the stage comes from NHTSA survival data instead.
  • Dropped: the seller's own 12-month sales slope. At a few orders a month it is mostly noise; Amazon market demand is used, and sales only as a check.
  • Not used yet: NHTSA complaint counts. Popular models get more complaints simply because more exist, so counts need dividing by fleet size first. That is the next step.
  • Kept out: margin. The rules use price gaps, and no cost or revenue figures are published.

6 · What to do, in order

01

Stop reordering the 9 in-stock Exit lines

Mostly 1990s–2000s fitments with below-median demand. Hold price, sell what's there, and don't reorder; clear remaining units once ad and storage cost per sale passes their margin.

Frees
Cash and warehouse space for Invest lines
Measure
Days of inventory on these lines, monthly
02

Reprice or exit the 8 NOx sensors

Diesel trucks are the slowest segment to electrify, so the category is worth keeping. The problem is price, not lifecycle: every listing sits 72%–273% above a same-part rival.

Decision
Margin at the rival's price; if negative, exit
Measure
First NOx orders after repricing
03

Harvest LESTER 8301: relist it ③ on the matrix

GM full-size trucks and SUVs 2007–2014. 41% of that fleet is still running, it is the highest-demand alternator line (2,636 clicks a month), and the seller's old listing was priced within 2% of the market.

Stage
Tail, high demand → Harvest
Measure
Orders within 60 days of relisting
04

Source the top Invest gaps first ④ on the matrix

19 Invest lines are not in the catalog. The largest are 2010s light trucks and SUVs: LESTER 11570 (Dodge Grand Caravan/Journey, Chrysler Town & Country 3.6L 2011-2020), Silverado/Sierra 1500-3500, Suburban, Yukon 2014-2019, and Chevy Traverse/GMC Acadia/Buick Enclave 3.6L 2007-2017. Check interchange with existing parts before tooling anything new.

Measure

This builds on Amazon Category Intelligence, which fixed how listings are found. This page decides which listings deserve stock and money over the next three to five years.

How it's built

Method

  • Lines: competitor part numbers (alternators, NOx sensors) and unmet-demand search intents (starters) from Amazon Product Opportunity Explorer, already matched to the seller's catalog by LESTER / OE number.
  • Age: model-year range parsed from each fitment; reference year 2026; survival looked up at the midpoint age.
  • Segment: passenger car vs. light truck / SUV from the first listed vehicle; heavy-duty trucks on the light-truck curve.
  • Decision: stage × demand rank (median split within each category), with a price override at 50% above a same-part rival.

Limitations

  • NHTSA's survival schedules are from 2006 and national; regional fleets (and modern cars) last longer.
  • Midpoint age simplifies long model-year ranges, such as 2009–2020.
  • Amazon demand covers Amazon only, not shops or other marketplaces.
  • The 50% price threshold and median split are judgment calls; both are one line in the SQL.
