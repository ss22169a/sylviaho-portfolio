E-commerce Funnel & Attribution Analysis with SQL

Where does paid traffic leak on its way to an Amazon order, how much of the revenue did the ads actually cause, and where should the next dollar go?

ContextU.S. automotive aftermarket seller on Amazon
WindowOct 7 – Nov 15, 2026 (40 days)
StackDuckDB SQL · Windsor.ai · Google Ads · Seller Central
GrainAd group × day · ASIN × day · order line

The short answer

The ads successfully acquired traffic, yet the majority of capital was allocated to unproven search intent; furthermore, a substantial share of won orders suffered from stock-out cancellations, and the revenue the ads actually caused covered only 38% of the break-even bar. The fix is not a bigger budget. It is four changes in where the money goes and how success is measured.

2 · Attribution: four lenses, not one ratio

Blended return65%of break-even. Revenue ÷ spend, crediting the ads with every sale, including sales that would have happened anyway.
Incremental return38%of break-even. Revenue above the 28-day pre-ad baseline ÷ spend. Weekly orders rose from 1.3 to 1.9; break-even needs 3.3.
Coverage4 of 11shipped orders had a vehicle-specific ad group for the same segment live in the 3 days before purchase. The other 7 came from segments with no specific ad running.
Seasonality check5 → 11shipped orders in the same 40 days, 2025 vs. 2026. Some lift is real, but with orders in single digits, a single bulk order swings the result.

The restructure moved traffic, not orders

On Oct 21 the account moved from generic broad keywords landing on the store home page (S1) to category and vehicle keywords landing on store category pages (S2). A handful of vehicle groups added in November land directly on one product page, but they have received under 2% of spend so far.

S2 delivered four times the product-page traffic at a quarter of the cost, yet each session converted at a quarter of the rate. One S1 order was a 3-unit bulk purchase, so S1's lead is not statistically reliable either. Next-day clicks correlate with sessions (r = 0.51), but daily spend does not (r = 0.01): paying more per click did not buy more visits.

Where the product-page sessions landed

344 sessions spread across 111 products; 68 of them got exactly one visit. 42% of sessions landed on products that have never sold, and only 8 products converted.

The single most-visited product (an F-150 alternator) drew 30 sessions and no orders. Its history shows 6 cancellations and a return for low charging voltage, so the ads were sending buyers to the listing least likely to close.

5 · Four goals worth rethinking the plan for

01

Fund demand that already exists

71% of spend buys searches that name no vehicle, and 6.5% goes to a sensor line that has never sold (priced 2–3× above aftermarket). Meanwhile the two segments that make up 59% of proven demand get almost nothing.

Now22%Target≥ 60%of spend on vehicle or part-number groups mapped to segments with shipped orders
02

Fix the leak before opening the tap

Every cancelled order is demand already paid for. 2026 stock-outs alone cost almost as much as the ads brought in. Ad groups should pause automatically when the product they promote runs low, and slow-to-ship parts should stop offering expedited delivery.

Now41%Target< 10%cancellation rate; zero ads on products with less than 3 units in stock
03

Buy intent, not traffic

Raising product-page traffic fourfold cut conversion to a quarter. Each ad group should land on the few products it names, choosing listings with stock and clean return histories, and be judged on orders per session, not sessions per click.

Now2.3%Target2×orders per product-page session, tracked per ad group
04

Measure increments, not ratios

The blended ratio flatters the ads (it reaches 65% of break-even, against 38% incremental), and 11 orders cannot settle the question either way. Only 2 of 15 ad groups carry an Amazon Attribution tag. Tag every ad group, then run a two-week ads-off holdout.

Now2 / 15Target15 / 15ad groups tagged; go/no-go rule: ≥ 3.3 orders a week

How it's built

Model

  • Staging: Google Ads by ad group × day × network × device (TWD → USD at the company booking rate), Amazon Sales & Traffic by ASIN × day, and order lines with status and returns.
  • Marts: funnel by regime, ad-group economics, network leakage, ASIN traffic quality, cancellation causes, incrementality, budget–demand alignment, coverage, response lag, and break-even.
  • Segments: every ad group and every order is mapped to the same vehicle segment, so spend and demand can be compared without a click-level join.

Limits, stated plainly

  • Order counts are small, so these findings set direction rather than prove effects; the holdout in goal 04 is how to prove them.
  • The pre-ad baseline is a single 28-day window, and the ad-platform API returns only the last 30 days.
  • Dollar values are withheld; shares and indexes come from the same queries.

Two of the queries

-- A1. Incrementality vs. the 28-day pre-ad baseline
WITH base AS (SELECT revenue_usd / days AS rev_day FROM stg_baseline),
     agg  AS (SELECT regime, COUNT(*) AS days,
                     SUM(revenue_usd) AS rev, SUM(spend_usd) AS spend
              FROM stg_daily GROUP BY regime)
SELECT regime,
       rev / spend                          AS blended_return,
       (rev - b.rev_day * days) / spend     AS incremental_return
FROM agg, base b;

-- A3. Does the budget follow proven demand?
SELECT segment,
       spend_usd / SUM(spend_usd) OVER ()   AS spend_share,
       demand_usd / SUM(demand_usd) OVER () AS demand_share
FROM segment_spend FULL JOIN segment_demand USING (segment);
