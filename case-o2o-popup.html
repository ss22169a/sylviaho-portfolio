An E-commerce Brand Opens Its First Store: How to Connect the OMO Experience?

An online-only handbag brand wanted physical retail. The risk: stores that take sales from the web shop instead of adding to them. The pop-up was designed as one stage of the online funnel, not a separate channel.

RoleCo-Founder
Period~3 months from Jun 2023
ToolsGA4 · Hotjar · SEMrush · LINE · Shopify
Result+2,000 members (120% of target)

Conclusion

Young consumers of higher-priced handbags research digitally, seek tactile validation, and purchase through whichever channel offers the lowest friction. So the pop-up's job was to turn online attention into a visit and a membership, and send people back online to buy. It signed up 2,000 new members (120% of target) and sold NT$2.5M in its first month. Online sales grew with it: store visits raised online exposure, and shoppers who couldn't find their size or color in store were sent to the web shop.

1 · What the research said

Market trendTrust gapOnline shopping kept growing, but for higher-priced items buyers still wanted to see the product first. A physical touchpoint lowers the risk of buying.
Decision path3 stepsResearch online → try offline → buy online. The store sits in the middle of the journey, not at the end.
CompetitorsOnline onlyMost rivals competed on online discounts and had no in-person experience, so a store experience was the open gap.

2 · The pop-up as a funnel stage

Fast reach → precise conversion → member loyalty
  1. Online → storeInviteWebsite and social posts, LINE and email invitations with a store-only offer, partner-brand and media features, paid ads.
  2. In storeExperienceTry the bags, member sign-up at the counter, leather-care workshops, event-only offers.
  3. Store → onlineReturnPost-visit offers and after-care content bring members back to the online shop for the next purchase.
The branch that protected online sales

When a size or color wasn't in the store, staff sent the shopper to the web shop instead of losing the sale. The store fed the online funnel rather than competing with it.

4 · How many counters to reach NT$100M?

Planning model · indexed, absolute values withheld

One counter, as a share of its revenue

Gross margin (price at 2–3× cost)≈ 60%
Department-store commission (assumption)− 30%
Counter staff− 11%
Marketing, supplies, card fees− 6%
Net margin, average counter≈ 13%

Counters bill on commission, not rent, so most costs move with sales. Staff is the one fixed cost, which is why stronger counters earn a higher margin (about 16% at 1.5× the average).

Counters needed for NT$100M a year

8.3counters
5 open in my tenure

At the average counter, physical retail alone needs roughly 8–9 counters to pass NT$100M a year. Five were operating when I left; the brand later reached that 8–9 count. Counters still in negotiation at the time are not counted as mine.

5 · What I would measure differently

An honest look at the ad data

Re-auditing the Google Ads account for this retrospective (Dec 2021 – Jul 2024, the oldest range Google still shows), I discovered that page views had been configured as a primary conversion, artificially inflating the ad "conversion rate" to 13% when it mostly counted people who opened a page. I don't use that figure here.

What I'd set up today

  • Purchases only as the primary conversion; page views and add-to-cart as secondary.
  • A store-only code or QR link per pop-up, so online orders that started in the store can be counted.
  • A before / during / after view of online sales for each pop-up, to prove the store adds to the web shop rather than taking from it.
